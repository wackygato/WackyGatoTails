Weaker students in VWO may drop to HAVO, while strong VMBO students might move to HAVO. This **allows for movement and reassessment rather than permanent early placement**.

**Belgium**

**Switzerland's system varies by canton, but many use two-stage placement.** Students are initially sorted at age 12, but **reassessment and transfers between tracks occur around age 14-15** at the end of lower secondary school, allowing some flexibility for late bloomers.

**England (UK)**
**England historically used the 11+ exam for grammar school placement**, but this system has largely been replaced by comprehensive schools. However, **grammar schools that remain typically select at age 11, and some students can enter grammar schools at age 13 or 14** through late-entry exams, providing a second opportunity for selection.

**Singapore**
**Singapore's system is largely fixed at age 12 with the PSLE (Primary School Leaving Exam)**, which streams students into Express, Normal Academic, or Normal Technical tracks. However, **students can transition between tracks (e.g., from Normal to Express) around age 14-15** based on secondary school performance, though this requires strong performance and approval.

---

## Why Two-Stage Systems?

**Two-stage tracking addresses criticisms of early placement by:**
- Allowing students whose abilities develop at different rates to move to more challenging (or appropriate) programs
- Reducing the impact of initial assessment errors or socioeconomic disadvantages at age 11
- Providing motivation for students in lower tracks by showing advancement is possible
- Reflecting research suggesting that **academic potential is not always evident at age 10-11**

However, **transfers in practice are often limited**—movement is easier upward (for high-achieving students) than downward, and some students face social or curricular barriers to switching tracks. The Netherlands and Germany are generally considered to have the **most permeable two-stage systems**, while countries like China and South Korea have more rigid, single-point-of-entry systems with little mid-stream movement.
